## 1. Parliament of India

Parliament consists of the President, the Lok Sabha (House of the People) and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States). The President is an integral part of Parliament even though the President does not sit in either House. A bill passed by both Houses becomes an Act only after it receives presidential assent.

The maximum strength of the Rajya Sabha is fixed at 250 members. Of these, 238 are representatives of the States and Union Territories, elected indirectly, and 12 are nominated by the President from persons having special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service. Rajya Sabha is a permanent body and is not subject to dissolution; one-third of its members retire every second year.

The Lok Sabha is elected directly by the people on the basis of universal adult franchise. Its normal term is five years from the date of its first meeting, but it can be dissolved earlier by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers. During a proclamation of national emergency, its term can be extended by a law of Parliament for one year at a time, but not beyond six months after the emergency ceases to operate.

### Money bills

A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha and only on the recommendation of the President. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides whether a bill is a Money Bill, and the Speaker's decision is final. The Rajya Sabha cannot reject or amend a Money Bill; it can only make recommendations and must return the bill within 14 days, after which it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses.

### Joint sitting

Article 108 provides for a joint sitting of both Houses to resolve a deadlock over an ordinary bill. The joint sitting is summoned by the President and presided over by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha. There is no provision for a joint sitting on a Money Bill or on a Constitution Amendment Bill. Joint sittings have been held only a few times, for example on the Dowry Prohibition Bill, the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill.

### Parliamentary committees

The Public Accounts Committee examines the annual audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General after they are laid before Parliament. By convention, its chairman is appointed from the Opposition. The Estimates Committee suggests economies in public expenditure and is drawn only from the Lok Sabha. Departmentally Related Standing Committees scrutinise the demands for grants of ministries and examine bills referred to them.

## 2. Federalism and Centre–State relations

The Constitution describes India as a "Union of States" rather than a federation. The States have no right to secede, and Parliament can alter the area, boundaries or names of States under Article 3 by a simple majority. Scholars describe the Indian system as quasi-federal, with a strong unitary bias.

Legislative powers are divided by the Seventh Schedule into three lists. The Union List covers subjects such as defence, foreign affairs, banking and atomic energy. The State List covers subjects such as police, public order, public health and agriculture. The Concurrent List covers subjects such as criminal law, marriage, education and forests. In case of conflict on a Concurrent List subject, the Union law prevails. Residuary powers — subjects not listed anywhere — are vested in Parliament under Article 248.

Parliament can legislate on a State List subject in the national interest if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by two-thirds of the members present and voting. Such a resolution remains in force for one year and can be renewed any number of times.

The Inter-State Council under Article 263 is a forum for consultation between the Centre and States. It was set up in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission. The Finance Commission under Article 280 is constituted every five years and recommends the distribution of the net proceeds of shareable central taxes between the Centre and the States.

## 3. Emergency provisions

Part XVIII of the Constitution (Articles 352 to 360) deals with emergencies. Three kinds of emergency are provided: national emergency, President's Rule in a State, and financial emergency.

A national emergency under Article 352 may be declared on the grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment of 1978 replaced the words "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion". The proclamation must be approved by both Houses within one month, by a special majority, and once approved it continues for six months at a time.

During a national emergency, Article 19 is suspended automatically when the emergency is declared on the ground of war or external aggression. The President can suspend the enforcement of other Fundamental Rights, but the 44th Amendment provides that the rights under Article 20 and Article 21 cannot be suspended even during an emergency.

President's Rule under Article 356 is imposed when the constitutional machinery in a State fails. It must be approved by Parliament within two months and can be extended up to a maximum of three years. In the S.R. Bommai case (1994), the Supreme Court held that the proclamation is subject to judicial review and that the floor of the Assembly is the proper place to test a government's majority.

A financial emergency under Article 360 can be declared if the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. It has never been declared so far. During it, the salaries of all government servants, including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, can be reduced.

## 4. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

Fundamental Rights are contained in Part III (Articles 12 to 35) and are justiciable. Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) are in Part IV (Articles 36 to 51) and are not enforceable by any court, yet they are fundamental in the governance of the country.

In the Minerva Mills case (1980), the Supreme Court held that the Constitution is founded on the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles, and that giving absolute primacy to one over the other would disturb the harmony of the Constitution.

Article 32 gives the right to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Article 32 the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. The Supreme Court can issue five kinds of writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto. Habeas corpus can be issued against both public authorities and private individuals, whereas mandamus cannot be issued against a private individual.

The Right to Education was inserted as Article 21A by the 86th Amendment of 2002. It makes free and compulsory education a fundamental right for all children aged six to fourteen years.

## 5. Basic structure doctrine

In the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), a thirteen-judge bench held by a 7:6 majority that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter its basic structure. The court did not give an exhaustive list of basic features. Later judgments have treated judicial review, the supremacy of the Constitution, secularism, federalism, the rule of law and free and fair elections as part of the basic structure.

The earlier Golaknath case (1967) had held that Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights at all. Parliament responded with the 24th Amendment, which affirmed its power to amend any part of the Constitution, and the Kesavananda judgment then limited that power through the basic structure test.

## 6. Constitutional bodies

The Election Commission is established under Article 324. It consists of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners as the President may fix. The Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court.

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) is appointed under Article 148 and audits the accounts of the Union and the States. The CAG holds office for six years or until the age of 65 years, whichever is earlier. The CAG is not eligible for any further office under the Government of India or of any State after ceasing to hold office.

The Union Public Service Commission is established under Article 315. Its chairman and members are appointed by the President and hold office for six years or until they attain the age of 65 years. The UPSC presents an annual report on its work to the President, which is laid before both Houses of Parliament.

The Attorney General of India is appointed under Article 76 and is the highest law officer of the country. The Attorney General has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses of Parliament but has no right to vote.

## 7. Local self-government

The 73rd Amendment of 1992 gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj institutions and added Part IX and the Eleventh Schedule, which lists 29 subjects. It provides for a three-tier system at the village, intermediate and district levels; States with a population not exceeding 20 lakh may skip the intermediate level.

At least one-third of the seats in panchayats are reserved for women. The State Election Commission conducts panchayat elections, and a State Finance Commission is constituted every five years to review the financial position of panchayats. The Gram Sabha consists of all persons registered in the electoral rolls of the village.

The 74th Amendment of 1992 did the same for urban local bodies: it added Part IXA and the Twelfth Schedule, which lists 18 functions, such as urban planning, water supply and fire services. It provides for three types of municipalities: nagar panchayats for areas in transition from rural to urban, municipal councils for smaller urban areas and municipal corporations for larger urban areas.

## 8. Anti-defection law

The Tenth Schedule was added by the 52nd Amendment of 1985 to curb political defections. A member is disqualified if he voluntarily gives up the membership of his party or votes contrary to the party whip without permission. The 91st Amendment of 2003 deleted the exception for a split of one-third of the legislature party; only a merger supported by two-thirds of the legislature party is now protected. The presiding officer decides questions of disqualification, and in Kihoto Hollohan (1992) the Supreme Court held that this decision is subject to judicial review.

## 9. The President and the Vice-President

The President is elected by an electoral college consisting of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States and of the Union Territories of Delhi and Puducherry. Nominated members do not take part in the presidential election. The election is held in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, and the voting is by secret ballot.

The President holds office for a term of five years and is eligible for re-election. The President can be removed by impeachment for violation of the Constitution under Article 61. The impeachment charges can be initiated by either House, must be signed by one-fourth of the members of that House, and require a fourteen days' notice. The resolution must be passed by a majority of two-thirds of the total membership of each House.

The ordinance-making power of the President under Article 123 can be exercised only when both Houses are not in session or when either House is not in session. An ordinance must be approved by Parliament within six weeks of its reassembly, otherwise it ceases to operate. In the D.C. Wadhwa case (1987), the Supreme Court held that the repeated re-promulgation of ordinances without placing them before the legislature is a fraud on the Constitution.

The pardoning power under Article 72 allows the President to grant pardons, reprieves, respites and remissions of punishment, and to suspend, remit or commute a sentence. It extends to sentences by a court martial and to cases of death sentence. The President exercises this power on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers. The Governor has a similar power under Article 161, but the Governor cannot pardon a death sentence, although the Governor can suspend, remit or commute it.

The Vice-President is the ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The Vice-President is elected by an electoral college consisting of members of both Houses of Parliament, including nominated members, but not the members of the State Legislative Assemblies. The Vice-President can be removed by a resolution of the Rajya Sabha passed by an effective majority and agreed to by the Lok Sabha; no formal impeachment is required.

## 10. The Governor and the State executive

The Governor is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal and holds office during the pleasure of the President. The same person can be appointed Governor of two or more States. The Constitution does not lay down any grounds for the removal of a Governor. In the B.P. Singhal case (2010), the Supreme Court held that a Governor cannot be removed in an arbitrary, capricious or unreasonable manner, merely because of a change of government at the Centre.

The Governor can reserve a bill passed by the State Legislature for the consideration of the President. Reservation is obligatory when the bill would endanger the position of the High Court. The discretionary powers of the Governor include the reservation of bills, recommending President's Rule, and the appointment of a Chief Minister when no party has a clear majority in the Assembly.

The Chief Minister is appointed by the Governor and the other ministers are appointed by the Governor on the advice of the Chief Minister. The total number of ministers, including the Chief Minister, in a State shall not exceed fifteen percent of the total strength of the Legislative Assembly; this limit was added by the 91st Amendment, and the number shall not be less than twelve.

## 11. The judiciary

The Supreme Court was inaugurated on 28 January 1950. Judges of the Supreme Court are appointed by the President. Since the Second Judges case (1993), appointments have been made on the recommendation of a collegium headed by the Chief Justice of India. In the Fourth Judges case (2015), the Supreme Court struck down the 99th Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) as violative of the independence of the judiciary, which is part of the basic structure.

A judge of the Supreme Court holds office until the age of 65 years. A judge can be removed by an order of the President only after an address by each House of Parliament, supported by a special majority, on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The procedure is laid down in the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.

The original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 131 covers disputes between the Centre and one or more States, or between two or more States. The advisory jurisdiction under Article 143 allows the President to seek the opinion of the Supreme Court on any question of law or fact of public importance; the opinion is not binding on the President.

Public interest litigation was developed by the Supreme Court in the early 1980s, with Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer among its pioneers. It relaxed the rule of locus standi, so that any public-spirited person can approach the court on behalf of those who cannot do so themselves. Even a letter or a postcard has been treated as a writ petition.

## 12. Amendment of the Constitution

Article 368 in Part XX deals with the power of Parliament to amend the Constitution. An amendment bill can be introduced in either House of Parliament, but not in the State Legislatures. It does not require the prior permission of the President. Some provisions can be amended by a special majority of Parliament alone, while provisions relating to the federal structure, such as the election of the President or the representation of States in Parliament, also require ratification by half of the State Legislatures by a simple majority.

The President must give assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill; the 24th Amendment of 1971 made this obligatory. The President cannot withhold assent or return the bill for reconsideration. Several provisions, such as the creation of new States under Article 3, the abolition or creation of Legislative Councils and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules, can be changed by a simple majority of Parliament and are not deemed to be amendments under Article 368.

## 13. Fundamental Duties

Fundamental Duties were added by the 42nd Amendment of 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. They are contained in Article 51A in Part IVA. Originally there were ten duties; the eleventh duty, to provide opportunities for education to one's child between six and fourteen years, was added by the 86th Amendment of 2002. The duties are inspired by the Constitution of the former USSR, and like the Directive Principles they are not directly enforceable by courts.

The duties include respecting the Constitution, the National Flag and the National Anthem, cherishing the noble ideals of the freedom struggle, protecting the sovereignty and integrity of India, promoting harmony and renouncing practices derogatory to the dignity of women, protecting the natural environment, developing scientific temper and humanism, and safeguarding public property.

## 14. Elections and representation

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 deals with the actual conduct of elections, qualifications and disqualifications of members, corrupt practices and election disputes. Election disputes regarding Parliament and State Legislatures are decided by the High Court, with an appeal to the Supreme Court. Election disputes regarding the President and the Vice-President are decided only by the Supreme Court.

The delimitation of constituencies is carried out by a Delimitation Commission whose orders have the force of law and cannot be called in question before any court. The 84th Amendment of 2001 froze the total number of Lok Sabha seats allotted to the States, based on the 1971 census, until the first census after 2026. The voting age was reduced from 21 to 18 years by the 61st Amendment of 1988.

The None Of The Above (NOTA) option was introduced on electronic voting machines following the Supreme Court judgment in the People's Union for Civil Liberties case (2013). Even if NOTA receives the highest number of votes, the candidate with the most votes among the contestants is declared elected.
