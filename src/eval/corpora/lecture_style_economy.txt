So first of all what is monetary policy. Monetary policy is the policy of the central bank, in India the Reserve Bank of India, to manage the supply of money and the cost of money, that is the interest rate, in order to keep prices stable while keeping in mind the objective of growth. Now since 2016 we have something called the flexible inflation targeting framework. Under this framework the government, in consultation with the RBI, sets an inflation target once every five years and the current target is four percent consumer price inflation with a band of plus minus two percent, so the tolerance band is two to six percent. Remember this, the target is on CPI combined, not on WPI, many students make this mistake in the exam. Now who decides the policy rate. Earlier the RBI governor used to decide alone but now we have the Monetary Policy Committee, the MPC. The MPC has six members, three are from the RBI including the governor, and three are external members appointed by the central government. Each member has one vote and in case of a tie the governor has a casting vote, okay, the governor has the second or casting vote. The MPC meets at least four times a year and in practice it meets six times, every two months. Then if inflation stays outside the band for three consecutive quarters, that is considered a failure to meet the target and the RBI has to write a report to the government explaining the reasons and the remedial actions. Now let us come to the tools. The most important tool is the repo rate. Repo rate is the rate at which the RBI lends short term money to commercial banks against government securities as collateral. When RBI increases the repo rate, borrowing becomes costlier for banks, they pass it on to customers, loans become expensive, demand comes down and inflation is controlled, this is called transmission. The reverse repo rate was the rate at which the RBI absorbs liquidity from banks, but since 2022 the floor of the corridor is the standing deposit facility, the SDF, which allows the RBI to absorb liquidity without giving any collateral, so this is a very important change, note it down. Then we have the marginal standing facility, MSF, which is a penal window where banks can borrow overnight from RBI when the interbank liquidity dries up, and MSF rate is above the repo rate, so the corridor is SDF at the bottom, repo in the middle, MSF at the top. Now quantitative tools, the cash reserve ratio and the statutory liquidity ratio. Cash reserve ratio, CRR, is the share of net demand and time liabilities that a bank must keep as cash with the RBI, and banks earn no interest on CRR balances. Statutory liquidity ratio, SLR, is the share of net demand and time liabilities that a bank must maintain in liquid assets like cash, gold and approved government securities with itself, not with the RBI. So CRR is kept with RBI, SLR is kept with the bank itself, this is a classic prelims trap. Then open market operations, OMO, where RBI buys or sells government securities in the market, if RBI buys securities it injects liquidity, if it sells it absorbs liquidity. Now inflation, let us understand types. Demand pull inflation happens when aggregate demand is higher than aggregate supply, too much money chasing too few goods. Cost push inflation happens when the cost of production goes up, for example crude oil prices increase or wages increase, and then producers pass on the cost to consumers. And then there is something called core inflation, core inflation excludes the volatile items, food and fuel, so it shows the underlying trend in prices. Headline inflation includes everything. Now an important concept for mains, stagflation, stagflation is the situation where you have high inflation along with low growth and high unemployment at the same time, India faced something like this risk after oil shocks, and the problem is that monetary policy cannot fix both together, if you raise rates to control inflation then growth falls further. Next, what is the difference between CPI and WPI. The consumer price index measures retail prices paid by households and it includes services, it is released by the National Statistics Office under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. The wholesale price index measures prices at the wholesale level, it does not include services, and it is released by the Office of the Economic Adviser in the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade. Food has a much higher weight in CPI than in WPI, that is why food price shocks hit CPI much more. Then one more thing, the base year, the current CPI base year is 2012 and the WPI base year is 2011-12, there is talk of revising both, so just keep an eye on the news. Okay then quickly the fiscal side, because examiners love to mix fiscal and monetary. Fiscal policy is the government's policy on taxation and expenditure and it is decided by the government through the budget, not by the RBI. The fiscal deficit is the difference between total expenditure and total receipts excluding borrowings, so it tells you how much the government needs to borrow. The FRBM Act, the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act of 2003, was brought to institutionalise fiscal discipline, and the N.K. Singh committee later recommended debt as the primary anchor of fiscal policy. Okay so welcome back, this is part two and today we are covering banking and the external sector, so take out your notebooks. First, non performing assets, NPA. A loan becomes a non performing asset when the interest or the principal remains overdue for more than ninety days, ninety days is the number you must remember. Before ninety days, banks classify stressed accounts as special mention accounts, SMA zero, SMA one and SMA two, depending on how many days the payment is overdue. Then NPAs are further classified as substandard assets, doubtful assets and loss assets depending on how long they have remained non performing. Now why did India have a twin balance sheet problem. The twin balance sheet problem means that both the banks and the corporates had stressed balance sheets at the same time, the corporates were over leveraged and could not repay, and the banks were sitting on large NPAs and could not lend, this term was popularised in the Economic Survey of 2016-17. To deal with this the government brought the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code in 2016. Under the IBC the resolution process has to be completed within a time limit of one hundred and eighty days, which can be extended by ninety days, and the total outer limit including litigation is three hundred and thirty days. The adjudicating authority for companies is the National Company Law Tribunal, the NCLT, and appeals go to the NCLAT. Then there was the recapitalisation of public sector banks and the merger of banks, for example in 2019 ten public sector banks were merged into four. Now Basel norms, Basel norms are international banking regulations issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, which sits at the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, Switzerland. Basel three focuses on capital adequacy, leverage and liquidity, and it introduced the liquidity coverage ratio, which requires banks to hold enough high quality liquid assets to survive a thirty day stress scenario. In India the RBI requires a capital to risk weighted assets ratio of nine percent, which is higher than the Basel requirement of eight percent, so India is stricter, note that. Now small finance banks and payments banks, a very common prelims question. Payments banks can accept deposits up to a limit, currently two lakh rupees per customer, they can issue debit cards but they cannot issue credit cards and they cannot give loans. Small finance banks on the other hand can give loans, and they must lend seventy five percent of their adjusted net bank credit to priority sector, and fifty percent of their loan portfolio must be loans of up to twenty five lakh rupees. Then priority sector lending, for scheduled commercial banks the overall priority sector target is forty percent of adjusted net bank credit, and within that there is a sub target for agriculture of eighteen percent. Okay now let us move to the external sector. The balance of payments has two main accounts, the current account and the capital account. The current account records trade in goods, trade in services, primary income like interest and dividends, and secondary income like remittances. India usually runs a deficit in merchandise trade but a surplus in services trade, mainly because of software exports, and India is the largest recipient of remittances in the world. The capital account records foreign direct investment, foreign portfolio investment, external commercial borrowings and so on. Now the difference between FDI and FPI, students always ask this. Foreign direct investment is investment with a lasting interest and some control in the enterprise, and in India an investment of ten percent or more of the equity of a listed company is treated as FDI, while below ten percent it is treated as foreign portfolio investment. FPI is called hot money because it can leave the country very quickly, which can cause volatility in the rupee and in the stock market. Then exchange rate, India follows a managed float, which means the rupee is market determined but the RBI intervenes in the foreign exchange market to curb excessive volatility, it does not target a particular level. When the rupee depreciates, our exports become cheaper for foreigners and our imports become costlier, so in theory depreciation helps exports but it also imports inflation, especially through crude oil. Then the real effective exchange rate, REER, is the weighted average of the rupee against a basket of currencies adjusted for inflation differentials, and if REER is above one hundred the rupee is considered overvalued. Then foreign exchange reserves, they consist of foreign currency assets, gold, special drawing rights and the reserve tranche position with the IMF, and the largest component is foreign currency assets. Then quickly, current account convertibility versus capital account convertibility. India has full current account convertibility since 1994, after accepting Article eight of the IMF articles of agreement, but India has only partial capital account convertibility, the Tarapore committee had recommended a phased move towards full capital account convertibility with some preconditions like a low fiscal deficit and low inflation. And the last point for today, the liberalised remittance scheme, LRS, allows resident individuals to remit up to two hundred and fifty thousand US dollars per financial year for permitted current and capital account transactions.
